The Wedding DJ Timeline

When to book, when to make the decisions, and what actually needs to be in your DJ's hands before the day. Written from 900+ events on the other side of the booth.

Most of the stress around wedding music isn't the music. It's the timing — couples wait too long to book, then rush the decisions that matter and spend the last month worrying about the ones that don't.

Here's the order that actually works. It's built backwards from the day, not forwards from the venue tour.

12 months out — book the DJ

Do this first

  • Book the date. Saturdays in May, June, September, and October go first. If you want a specific DJ — not just "a DJ" — this is the month.
  • Ask what the price includes. Ceremony sound, wireless mics, uplighting, extra hours, and travel are where quotes quietly differ. A number without a list isn't a quote. Ours are posted here.
  • Ask about backup gear. Any DJ who doesn't carry a spare mixer or laptop has never had one die at 8:40 PM.

9–10 months out — decide what kind of party it is

The big creative call

  • Formal and elegant, or a full dance-floor party? Both are correct — but they're different shows, and the DJ builds the night around the answer.
  • Start two lists: must-play and do-not-play. The do-not-play list matters more than couples expect.
  • Think about the guests, not just yourselves. A floor full of 60-year-olds and a floor full of 25-year-olds need different songs to stand up.

6 months out — build the ceremony

Where the details live

  • Ceremony processional, bridal entrance, recessional, and any special moments (unity candle, sand, memorial). Four songs, decided early.
  • Confirm who's officiating and whether they need a microphone. This is the single most common ceremony problem, and it's free to solve in advance.
  • First dance, parent dances, and whether you're doing a grand entrance.

3 months out — the timeline

Draft the running order

  • Draft the reception order: entrance, first dance, toasts, dinner, cake, bouquet, open dancing, last song.
  • Your DJ should be able to tell you what usually runs long here. Dinner always runs long.
  • Decide who speaks at toasts and give them a time limit. Two minutes is generous.

1 month out — the planning call

One conversation, everything on the table

  • Walk the whole timeline start to finish with your DJ, out loud.
  • Share the final music lists and the pronunciations of every name being announced.
  • Confirm venue load-in time, power, parking, and where the DJ sets up. Venues have rules; the DJ needs to hear them from you, not at load-in.

2 weeks out — confirm, don't change

Lock it

  • Final guest count and final timeline to every vendor, including the DJ.
  • Send the photo list to the photographer and the song list to the DJ. Don't swap them.
  • Resist rewriting the timeline. Late changes are where days go sideways.

Week of — hand it off

Your job is to show up

  • Confirm arrival time and a phone number that someone will actually answer on the day — ideally not the bride's.
  • Tell your DJ who the point person is: the planner, the maid of honor, the venue coordinator. One name.
  • Then stop managing it. A good DJ runs the room from here.

The day of

Setup happens before your guests arrive — typically two hours ahead for a full ceremony-and-reception. Sound check is done in an empty room, not in front of your grandmother. The DJ stays out of the photographer's shot, keeps the toasts on schedule, and reads the floor instead of a playlist.

Want the short version?

We send every couple a planning form and get on the phone once, about a month out. That one call replaces forty emails — and it's the reason the timeline holds together on the day.

Check Your Date

Questions couples actually ask

How far in advance should we book a wedding DJ? Six to twelve months for a Saturday in peak season. Off-season and Friday/Sunday dates can be booked much closer — three or four months is often fine.
Do we need to give the DJ a full playlist? No. Give a must-play list, a do-not-play list, and a handful of songs that define the vibe. A DJ who needs 200 songs picked for them isn't reading the room.
Who runs the timeline on the day? Your DJ should, alongside the venue coordinator. The couple shouldn't be the ones tracking whether the cake is ready.
What if our ceremony and reception are in different rooms? Two setups, one system, one operator. It's a planning detail, not a problem — but say it up front so the quote reflects reality.
Can we meet before booking? Yes. A short call is usually enough — you'll know within ten minutes whether the fit is right.
